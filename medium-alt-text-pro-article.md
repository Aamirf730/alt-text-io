# The Image Accessibility Task Everyone Puts Off

## How Alt Text Pro helps WordPress teams write better image descriptions without turning accessibility into another full-time job

![A content creator uses a laptop interface that transforms a landscape image into a written description.](assets/medium-alt-text-pro-hero.png)

*Turning image accessibility from a backlog into a practical publishing workflow.*

There is a small field in WordPress that is easy to ignore.

You see it every time you upload an image: **Alternative Text**.

On a new site with a handful of images, filling it in seems simple enough. But websites rarely stay small. A blog grows into hundreds of posts. An online store adds new products every week. A redesign brings years of old media into a fresh WordPress installation.

Before long, the Media Library is full of images named `IMG_4821.jpg`, `final-banner-v3.png`, and `product-shot-07.webp`, many of them with no useful description at all.

At that point, alt text stops feeling like a writing task and starts feeling like a backlog.

That is the problem we built [Alt Text Pro](https://www.alt-text.pro/) to solve.

## Alt text is small, but its absence is not

Alt text gives an image a text alternative. For someone using a screen reader, it can provide the information that sighted visitors receive from the image. If an image fails to load, alt text can also preserve some of its meaning.

It matters for search, too. [Google says it uses alt text](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/google-images), along with page content and computer vision, to understand what an image is about. That does not make alt text a magic ranking switch, but it does make a clear, relevant description part of a well-maintained website.

The real purpose, however, is simpler: if an image contributes meaning to a page, that meaning should not disappear for people who cannot see it.

The difficult part is doing this consistently.

A blogger may upload five images for a single article. A WooCommerce store may have a main product photo, a gallery, color variations, packaging shots, and lifestyle images for every item. An agency may inherit a client site with thousands of attachments collected over several years.

Writing one useful description takes only a moment. Writing several thousand of them is a different kind of problem.

## Why the usual shortcuts do not work

When the backlog becomes large, people understandably look for shortcuts. The most common one is to use the image filename as alt text.

That usually produces something like:

> blue-shoe-final-2

It may contain a keyword, but it does not communicate much to a person.

Another shortcut is to paste the same phrase into every related image:

> Buy blue shoes online

Repeated across a product gallery, this becomes noisy for screen-reader users and starts to look more like keyword stuffing than a genuine description.

Good alt text should be brief, specific, and appropriate to the image’s purpose on the page. A useful description might be:

> Navy suede derby shoe shown from the side

That is clearer because it describes what matters without trying to turn the alt field into an advertisement.

There is another important detail: not every image needs a written description. If an image is purely decorative or repeats information already available nearby, [W3C accessibility guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/decision-tree/) may call for an empty alt attribute so a screen reader can skip it. Complex charts may need a longer explanation in the surrounding page content.

In other words, filling every alt field with something is not the same as making a site accessible.

## We wanted the first draft to be the easy part

Alt Text Pro uses AI vision to analyze an image and produce a useful starting description. It works inside WordPress, where the image and its alt text already live.

The workflow is intentionally straightforward:

1. Install the Alt Text Pro plugin.
2. Connect it to an Alt Text Pro account.
3. Generate alt text for an individual image, a post or page, or a group of missing items in the Media Library.
4. Review the result, edit it if needed, and save it in WordPress.

This matters because accessibility tools are most useful when they fit the way people already work. Exporting a media list to a spreadsheet, sending images to a separate service, and pasting descriptions back into WordPress creates more work around the work.

With Alt Text Pro, a site owner can begin with one image. Once the results feel right, they can work through a post, a WooCommerce product, or a larger Media Library backlog.

The plugin can also skip images that already have alt text, helping teams focus their time and credits on the gaps instead of replacing work that has already been reviewed.

## AI can see the image. You understand the page.

This is the part of the conversation about AI-generated alt text that deserves more honesty.

An image does not always have one universally correct description.

Imagine a photograph of a glass jar on a kitchen counter. In a recipe, the important detail may be that the jar contains overnight oats topped with berries. In an article about sustainable packaging, the important detail may be that the food is stored in a reusable glass container.

The pixels are the same. The purpose is different.

AI is good at identifying visible content and turning it into a clear first draft. A person still understands why the image was placed on that particular page.

That is why Alt Text Pro keeps the generated text editable. The goal is not to remove people from the process. It is to remove the blank field and the repetitive work that prevents people from starting.

A useful review often takes only a few seconds:

- Does the description match the image?
- Does it communicate what matters in this context?
- Does it repeat nearby text unnecessarily?
- Is the image actually decorative?
- Has a keyword been included naturally, or forced into the sentence?

This combination of fast generation and human context is more practical than either extreme: writing every description from scratch or publishing every AI suggestion without looking at it.

## The WordPress backlogs this can help with

Different websites accumulate missing alt text in different ways.

For publishers, the problem is often historical. Years of articles may include featured images and in-post photography added by many different editors.

For WooCommerce stores, it is a matter of volume. Product catalogs grow quickly, and image descriptions are easy to miss during launches, seasonal updates, and bulk imports.

For agencies, the challenge is consistency. Accessibility and SEO cleanups often happen near the end of a project, when deadlines are tight and the team is already juggling redirects, metadata, performance, and content QA.

For site owners migrating from another platform, imported images may arrive without useful attachment metadata at all.

Alt Text Pro supports individual image generation, post and page workflows, WooCommerce product images, and bulk processing for missing Media Library alt text. It also keeps generation history and credit usage visible inside WordPress, so the work does not feel like a black box.

## Accessibility is a practice, not a plugin setting

No plugin can guarantee that a website is accessible.

Accessibility includes structure, keyboard navigation, color contrast, forms, captions, clear language, and much more. Even within image accessibility, the right choice depends on whether an image is informative, functional, decorative, or complex.

Alt Text Pro addresses one specific and widespread part of that work: creating and maintaining useful text alternatives for WordPress images at a realistic scale.

That distinction is important. We would rather help teams build a better habit than promise a one-click compliance badge.

The habit can be simple:

- Generate alt text when new images are uploaded.
- Review it in the context of the page.
- Audit older content in manageable batches.
- Leave decorative images appropriately empty.
- Treat descriptions as content that can be improved over time.

When this becomes part of the publishing workflow, the backlog stops growing.

## Start with one image

If your WordPress Media Library contains hundreds of missing descriptions, you do not need to fix everything today.

Start with one important page. Choose an image that contributes meaning. Generate a description, read it in context, and adjust it until it says what a visitor needs to know.

Then do the next one.

The best accessibility improvements are often not dramatic redesigns. They are small, thoughtful changes made consistently. Alt text is one of those changes, and the right workflow can turn it from an item that is always postponed into a normal part of publishing.
